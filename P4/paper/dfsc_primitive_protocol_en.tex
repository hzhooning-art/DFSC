\begin{abstract}
Differentiable numerical components cross software and hardware interfaces in simulation and learning systems, but a successful call or autodiff trace does not show that their values, parameter derivatives, or execution policies are reliable. We develop an executable testing and qualification method for a batched component $y=P(x;\theta)$. Each evidence record fixes the operating domain, independent references, execution policies, provenance, tolerances, and scope before evaluation. Under the same synthetic ten-class catalogue, four cumulative strategies detected 3, 5, 8, and 10 classes; the complete record detected 200/200 injected faults and rejected 0/40 clean records. A PyTorch external-subject pilot increased detection from 25.00\% to 100\% over 240 injected trials. A matched value/property subset spanning NumPy, SciPy, and PyTorch increased detection from 28.57\% to 100\% over 252 injected trials and accepted 36/36 controls. Representation equivalence derived from SciPy \#8906 detected 32/32 variants missed by a weak padded example. Unchanged runners reproduced PyTorch \#80770 and SciPy \#8906 and \#15620 faults in official release wheels and accepted current fixes, completing three historical buggy/fixed pairs across two projects and three failure modes. The results support incremental executable evidence across controlled projects and real release-level defects, without implying broad field prevalence.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Engineering software now couples learned modules to propagators, matrix-function actions, integration steps, and structured residual blocks. These components move between APIs, command-line tools, devices, dtypes, and continuous-integration jobs. Automatic differentiation follows the executed graph \citep{baydin2018autodiff}, but does not remove discretization, truncation, or floating-point error. A component can satisfy a callable interface while violating numerical, unit, batch-isolation, or execution-policy requirements.

Such defects are often discovered after integration, when diagnosis, retraining, and repeated system tests are expensive. The missing interface is not another solver API. It is a portable statement of what was tested, on which operating domain, under which execution policy, and with what decision.

We address this need with an executable conformance specification. Its schema binds an operating domain and execution request to value, gradient, batch, repeatability, OOD, horizon, dtype/device, unit, provenance, calibration, and composition evidence. A stable record interface carries identity, version, provenance, execution, and decision fields; backend plugins supply the numerical controls required by a component class. Core, extended, and application profiles let a consumer request only the evidence needed at a particular integration boundary.

The work contributes (i) a versioned component-level evidence model and three cumulative qualification profiles, (ii) a reference implementation with canonical JSON, explicit requested-versus-observed execution fields, and legacy-record migration, and (iii) paired strategy comparisons on both a synthetic fault catalogue and an upstream-defect-derived case. We ask three empirical questions: \emph{RQ1}, which fault classes are added by numerical-property, execution-aware, and complete evidence strategies; \emph{RQ2}, does the ordering transfer from record mutations to external numerical interfaces; and \emph{RQ3}, can a representation-equivalence property expose a real historical defect pattern that a correct single example masks? The method is designed for research and engineering workflows that require portable, machine-checkable reliability evidence.

\section{Related Work and Positioning}
Differentiable programming has made numerical simulators and differential-equation solvers trainable components. Representative directions include automatic differentiation as a general computational technique \citep{baydin2018autodiff}, differentiable physical simulation \citep{hu2020difftaichi,holl2020control}, neural ordinary differential equations \citep{chen2018neuralode}, universal differential equations \citep{rackauckas2020universal}, physics-informed neural networks and their software ecosystems \citep{raissi2019pinn,lu2021deepxde}, and neural operators that learn maps between function spaces \citep{kovachki2023neuraloperator}. These methods demonstrate the utility of differentiating through scientific computation, but they do not by themselves define a common acceptance test for the value, parameter derivative, calibration, and reuse behavior of an individual numerical component.

Existing ecosystems operate at complementary levels. DeepXDE and TorchPhysics organize differential-equation models, training objectives, geometry, and parameter-identification workflows \citep{lu2021deepxde,tanyu2023deeppde}; the SciML/DifferentialEquations ecosystem exposes common solver interfaces and work--precision benchmarking across algorithms and applications \citep{rackauckas2019confederated}. Our unit of qualification is one executed primitive. Before integration into a model or solver, the protocol checks independent value and derivative references, dtype-specific gates, and selected compositions, then writes the evidence and any operating restrictions to a machine-readable record.

Engineering verification and validation (V\&V) evaluates a model or simulation against its requirements, intended use, referent data, uncertainty, and life-cycle evidence \citep{nasa2024std7009}. The present workflow addresses a smaller unit: an executed differentiable component. It checks the forward map, parameter-derivative path, batched and device behavior, and selected compositions under a declared protocol. A component-level \texttt{CONFORMANT} decision concerns the numerical software layer. Model form, uncertainty, and agreement with experiments remain end-to-end V\&V questions.

The standards literature supplies adjacent concepts but no ready-made record for differentiable numerical kernels. ISO/IEC 25010 defines software-product quality characteristics, while ISO/IEC 25023 defines measures used to evaluate product quality \citep{iso25010_2023,iso25023_2016}. ISO/IEC/IEEE 29119-2 specifies organizational and project test processes, and IEEE 1012 addresses system, software, and hardware V\&V \citep{iso29119_2_2021,ieee1012_2024}. Table~\ref{tab:standards-map} maps our component-level fields to these sources. The mapping establishes traceable design provenance and identifies the component-level concerns implemented by the schema.

Numerical analysis supplies the diagnostics used by the software gates. Forward error, backward error, conditioning, and floating-point stability describe different failure mechanisms \citep{higham2002accuracy}. Stable algorithms exist for matrix-exponential actions and Frechet derivatives \citep{almohy2011action,almohy2009frechet}, and derivative condition estimates quantify perturbation amplification \citep{higham2008functions,higham2014frechet}. Discretized fractional calculus \citep{lubich1986discretized}, Mittag--Leffler evaluation \citep{garrappa2015mittag,gorenflo2014mittag}, and fractional Laplacian definitions \citep{lischke2020fractional} provide corresponding references for fractional propagation. We use these established results to select independent evaluators, perturbation probes, and acceptance tolerances. Agreement between two autodiff traces is insufficient when both traces pass through the same inaccurate evaluator.

Our preceding DFSC study introduced an error-controlled differentiable Mittag--Leffler propagation component and its SciML interfaces \citep{hu2026dfsc}. That work asks how a particular fractional propagator can be evaluated and differentiated reliably. The present paper asks what evidence must accompany a differentiable component before exchange or admission into a workflow. MLSL supplies one of four test backends, but no MLSL algorithm or numerical result is claimed here as a new contribution. Table~\ref{tab:p1-p4-boundary} makes this boundary explicit. The reporting design follows reproducible-computing practice \citep{wilson2017goodenough}; SciPy and other standard software provide independent controls \citep{virtanen2020scipy}.

\section{Executable Conformance Specification}
In this paper, \emph{conformance} is the agreement of a component record with the selected profile and its declared gates. A \emph{specification} is the versioned record schema together with its validation rules; a \emph{profile} is a cumulative set of required evidence; a \emph{gate} is a predeclared Boolean criterion; and an \emph{evidence plugin} produces backend-specific measurements. A \emph{test action} executes one or more such measurements, whereas \emph{qualification} denotes the resulting profile-level decision. These terms define the decision semantics implemented and evaluated in this work.

Each backend exposes a batched map
\begin{equation}
    y=P(x;\theta),
\end{equation}
with an explicit input and parameter domain, output shape, dtype policy, device policy, and reference evaluator. The protocol does not require every primitive to share the same mathematics. It requires the evaluation record to preserve the domain on which each claim is valid.

The executable specification stored with a submitted primitive is
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{C}=\bigl(\mathcal{D}_{x},\mathcal{D}_{\theta},R,
  \boldsymbol{\tau},\mathcal{M},\mathcal{S},\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{E}\bigr),
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{D}_{x}$ and $\mathcal{D}_{\theta}$ are the declared state and parameter domains, $R$ is an independent reference evaluator, $\boldsymbol{\tau}$ contains gate tolerances, $\mathcal{M}$ records dtype/device and implementation metadata, $\mathcal{S}$ states known scope limits, $\mathcal{Q}$ records the frozen scope and required coverage anchors, and $\mathcal{E}$ contains the requested and observed execution properties, units, and provenance. A result without these fields can be retained for diagnosis but is nonconformant to the selected profile.

\subsection{Conformance profiles and versioning}
The \texttt{Core} profile requires value and directional-gradient accuracy, batch shape and independence, repeatability, at least eight cases, and nominal, boundary, and heterogeneous-batch anchors. \texttt{Extended} requires at least 16 cases and adds perturbation, execution-policy, long-horizon, OOD, dtype/device, unit, and resource evidence. \texttt{Application} requires at least 24 cases and adds an application-composition anchor, calibration, and composition evidence. The scope and sampling plan are frozen before candidate execution. Missing mandatory evidence, an unfrozen scope, or insufficient coverage is a nonconformance rather than an omitted score. The current schema is \texttt{DFSC-DNC-Conformance-v3}. Migration from v1 or v2 preserves the original evidence in provenance but marks the record for requalification; it cannot inherit a conformant decision without satisfying the v3 coverage rules.

Interoperability is defined at the record boundary. A consumer selects a profile and submits a record carrying an exact schema identifier. The reference validator accepts v3, explicitly migrates v1 or v2 evidence into a requalification record, and rejects unknown identifiers. Canonical JSON sorts object keys, uses compact separators and ASCII escapes, and is encoded as UTF-8 before a SHA-256 digest is computed. Array order remains significant. The demonstrated claim is limited to the Python API, command-line path, canonical JSON, and the tested PyTorch evidence plugins; JAX, Julia, and independent validators were not evaluated. Semantic changes require a new schema identifier and a declared migration rather than silent interpretation.

\subsection{Reliability dimensions}
Value fidelity is measured against a declared reference using absolute and relative errors and finite-value rates. For prediction $\hat y$ and reference $y^{\star}$, we use $e_{\mathrm{rel}}=\|\hat y-y^{\star}\|_2/(\|y^{\star}\|_2+\epsilon)$. Acceptance is based on the mixed criterion
\begin{equation}
  \|\hat y-y^{\star}\|_2 \leq
  \tau_{\mathrm{abs}}+\tau_{\mathrm{rel}}\|y^{\star}\|_2,
  \label{eq:mixed-gate}
\end{equation}
which remains meaningful near zero. For a normalized parameter direction $v$, the gradient check compares the autodiff directional derivative $g_{\mathrm{AD}}=\nabla_{\theta}P(x;\theta)^{\mathsf T}v$ with a central-difference estimate $g_{\mathrm{FD}}$ and reports $e_{\mathrm{grad}}=|g_{\mathrm{AD}}-g_{\mathrm{FD}}|/(|g_{\mathrm{FD}}|+\epsilon)$. The finite-difference step is swept around a dtype-scaled nominal value rather than selected from one favorable perturbation; a gradient gate passes only when an agreement region is observed. Differentiability is additionally assessed using finite-gradient rates. Calibration uses noisy observations and reports parameter error, convergence behavior, seed variation, and a matched classical baseline where available. Module reuse evaluates at least two compositions, including a neural module and a batched operator or physics objective. OOD, long-horizon, and batch/device tests expose boundary behavior rather than collapsing it into one score.

The tolerances in Eq.~\eqref{eq:mixed-gate} are declared before the audit and are tied to the reference accuracy, dtype, conditioning, and downstream task resolution. A practical calibration starts with reference-only and perturbation runs. The relative tolerance must exceed the combined reference uncertainty and dtype-dependent roundoff after the observed conditioning is taken into account. The absolute tolerance covers the near-zero regime and should not be smaller than the output resolution that matters to the downstream task. Float32 and float64 receive separate tolerance records. The selected pair is frozen before the candidate implementation is evaluated, and a sensitivity table reports whether modest threshold changes alter the status. For ill-conditioned maps, a condition estimate or perturbation study is recorded so that forward sensitivity is not misclassified as an implementation defect.

Each test dimension retains its measured status. The machine-readable profile decision is binary: \texttt{CONFORMANT} means that every mandatory item is present, the scope was frozen, profile coverage requirements are met, and all gates pass on that scope. \texttt{NONCONFORMANT} records a failed gate, execution mismatch, coverage failure, migration requiring requalification, or missing evidence. Scope restrictions remain explicit fields and cannot be hidden by an aggregate score. In prose, a conformant record with nonempty restrictions is reported as \emph{conformant with declared scope limits}; this phrase does not introduce a third machine state.

The protocol separates differentiation from numerical accuracy. Automatic differentiation differentiates the executed computational graph, whereas approximation and floating-point errors enter through the evaluator and its dtype. In schematic form,
\begin{equation}
 e_{\mathrm{grad}} \lesssim
 \kappa_{P'}\bigl(e_{\mathrm{eval}}+e_{\mathrm{AD}}+e_{\mathrm{ref}}\bigr),
 \label{eq:gradient-decomposition}
\end{equation}
where $e_{\mathrm{eval}}$ denotes the approximation error in the executed primitive, $e_{\mathrm{AD}}$ the differentiation-path error, and $e_{\mathrm{ref}}$ the error of the independent derivative check. Let $z=(x,\theta)$ and $G(z)=P'(z)$. We interpret the local derivative sensitivity as
\begin{equation}
 \kappa_{P'}(z;s_z)=\lim_{\delta\to0^+}\sup_{\|\Delta z\|\leq\delta s_z}
 \frac{\|G(z+\Delta z)-G(z)\|}{\delta\bigl(\|G(z)\|+\epsilon_0\bigr)},
 \label{eq:derivative-condition}
\end{equation}
with a declared perturbation scale $s_z>0$ and a fixed output-scale guard $\epsilon_0>0$. The inputs and derivative outputs are nondimensionalized, or scaled by recorded physical units, before this diagnostic is evaluated. Accordingly, $\epsilon_0$ has the same normalized units as $\|G(z)\|$; it is fixed from the independent reference resolution before the candidate implementation is run and is stored in the record. This quantity measures the conditioning of the derivative map. For matrix functions it can be evaluated or estimated through Fr\'echet derivatives \citep{higham2008functions,higham2014frechet}. Equation~\eqref{eq:gradient-decomposition} is a diagnostic decomposition, not a global error bound.

Two mechanisms require separate checks. Products of step Jacobians can amplify reverse sensitivities in a stiff system even when the forward trajectory is bounded. Nonnormal operators can exhibit transient growth that is not visible from their eigenvalues. We therefore pair perturbation probes with horizon-dependent tests. For a stable $p$th-order stepper with step size $h$ and $N$ steps, the accumulated local truncation contribution scales as $O(Nh^{p+1})$ before problem-dependent stability amplification. Floating-point accumulation is commonly $O(Nu)$ in unit roundoff $u$, although cancellation and conditioning may change this behavior \citep{higham2002accuracy}. The audit record stores dtype, horizon, step size or truncation budget, reference precision, and whether the gradient reference follows the executed path. Float32 and float64 retain separate accuracy records.

\section{Reference Software Architecture}
The reference implementation is provided in the \texttt{P4/dfsc\_protocol} package. Existing numerical test actions measure finite outputs, value error, directional-gradient agreement, batch behavior, device locality, and resource use. The new \texttt{conformance} module validates the profile, compares requested with observed execution, and checks mandatory evidence. \texttt{canonical\_json} removes serialization-order ambiguity, while \texttt{record\_digest} gives a stable content identifier. The same evaluator is exposed through a Python API and a command-line interface. Reports are generated as canonical JSON suitable for continuous-integration gates and exchange between tools.

Every experiment follows the same order. The executable specification and metadata are frozen before sampling. In-domain cases are evaluated against an independent reference, followed by value and directional-gradient gates, calibration and composition tests over fixed seeds, and one-factor OOD or long-horizon extensions. Performance is measured only for configurations with finite outputs that satisfy the numerical gates. The record contains aggregate statistics, the worst observed metric, failed cases, execution properties, and the profile decision.

\section{Experimental Setup}
The standards-facing evaluation has two parts. First, 40 randomized records test API--CLI equivalence, key-order invariance, evidence-preserving legacy migration, and mandatory requalification after migration. Ten mutation operators target truncation evidence, gradient connectivity, dtype, batch isolation, device locality, OOD scope, units, provenance, scope freezing, and minimum profile coverage. Each operator is applied 20 times, and 40 unmodified records estimate false rejection. Four predeclared strategies are replayed on these same records and mutations: envelope plus value/gradient checks; numerical properties, which add batch isolation, repeatability, OOD, and horizon evidence; execution-aware checks, which add dtype, device, and unit evidence; and the complete record, which also enforces provenance, frozen scope, and profile coverage. Fault class is the primary comparison unit. These deliberately minimal strategies isolate evidence increments and are not claimed to represent every implementation of unit, property-based, or metamorphic testing. Second, numerical experiments provide the measured evidence consumed by the profiles. They combine controlled low-dimensional components with a direct two-dimensional periodic heat-equation component. The four initial backends are \mlsl{}, matrix-exponential action, fixed-step linear RK4, and Logistic RK4; the two-dimensional extension uses a differentiable Fourier propagator. Unless otherwise stated, multi-seed experiments use five seeds. The common neural OOD task uses three seeds, 512 training tasks, 256 test tasks, two target times ($1.0$ and $1.5$), and two observation-noise levels ($0.003$ and $0.01$) on an RTX 5070 Laptop GPU. The PDE test uses five grids from $16^2$ to $256^2$, five seeds, and three horizons; a finite trigonometric expansion supplies a closed-form reference independent of the FFT implementation.

The external-subject pilot replays four cumulative strategies on three installed PyTorch interfaces: scalar special-function differentiation, matrix-exponential action, and a non-contiguous CUDA contraction. Eight behavior-fault families, ten seeds, and three interfaces give 240 injected trials; 30 unmodified cases are clean controls. Subject--fault pairs, rather than repeated seeds, form the 24 heterogeneity clusters. A separate historical-defect intake freezes upstream provenance and observations for two PyTorch reports and SciPy \#8906. For the latter, 32 surrogate variants first compare a padded example that masks the reported hard-coded 1x1 band-row choice with a mathematically equivalent conventional representation. Unchanged runners then compare official buggy wheels with installed fixed releases for this case, PyTorch \#80770, and SciPy \#15620. The last case tests a dtype relation: integer and floating inputs to \texttt{resample\_poly} must represent the same nonzero signal. Interpreter, wheel, source or fix-commit, command, output, and runner hashes are retained.

\section{Results}
The reported metrics have backend-specific meanings and scales. Comparative plots are interpreted against each backend's declared test and tolerance; timing values are descriptive when execution stacks differ. \subsection{Interface interoperability and fault detection}
All 40 randomized trials produced byte-identical canonical records through the Python API and command-line interface, and key reordering did not change the digest. Legacy migration preserved the submitted evidence in all 40 trials, while every migrated record was correctly marked for requalification rather than inheriting its earlier decision. The mutation study detected 200 of 200 injected faults. The Wilson 95\% interval for pooled detection is $[0.981,1.000]$. Each of the ten mutation categories was detected in 20 of 20 trials, with a category-wise lower bound of $0.839$. None of 40 clean records was rejected; the Wilson interval for false rejection is $[0,0.088]$. These intervals quantify finite repeated tests under the selected mutation model. They neither estimate field-defect prevalence nor establish detection of arbitrary faults.

The paired strategy comparison localizes the gain. Table~\ref{tab:strategy-comparison} shows a monotone increase from 3 to 10 detected fault classes as execution, provenance, and scope evidence are added. All four strategies accepted the 40 clean records, so the additional coverage did not trade against specificity on this test set.

\subsection{External interfaces and historical-defect-derived strategy}
On the 240 external-subject injected trials, detection increased from 25.00\% for a single-point test to 73.75\% after directional-gradient evidence, 99.17\% for the numerical-property suite, and 100\% for complete execution evidence. All strategies accepted the 30 clean controls. At the 24 subject--fault cluster level, the complete strategy detected every cluster; successive layers added 3, 7, 7, and 1 fully detected clusters without losing a previously detected cluster. Leave-one-interface-out detection remained 1.000 for the complete suite. These are controlled behavior mutations within one vendor project, not field-defect prevalence estimates.

The SciPy \#8906-derived comparison answers RQ3 on one historical defect family. The weak padded example detected 0/32 paired variants because its chosen representation happened to place the coefficient in the faulty hard-coded row. Requiring equal solutions for the padded and conventional representations detected 32/32 variants; current SciPy 1.18.0 satisfied the property in 32/32 controls. For PyTorch \#80770, the identical runner (SHA-256 \texttt{fdb828...014}) returned a zero \texttt{xlogy(0,2)} derivative in the official 1.11.0+cpu wheel and $\log 2$ with zero stored error in current PyTorch 2.11.0+cu128. For SciPy \#8906, a second unchanged runner (SHA-256 \texttt{ce901...bc8}) made the conventional 1x1 representation raise the reported \texttt{IndexError} in the official 1.14.1 wheel while the padded representation solved exactly; current 1.18.0 solved both exactly. For SciPy \#15620, a third runner (SHA-256 \texttt{0a7a72...f79}) produced all-zero outputs for both integer dtypes in official 1.14.1 although the floating reference reached 3.001552; current 1.18.0 matched the float64 reference with zero maximum error. All six role checks passed, completing three historical buggy/fixed environment pairs across two projects.

A matched cross-project subset applied seven output/execution fault families to \texttt{numpy.linalg.solve}, \texttt{scipy.linalg.expm}, and \texttt{torch.lgamma}. On 252 injected trials, detection increased from 28.57\% for a single point to 57.14\% for full-batch values, 85.71\% for numerical properties, and 100\% for execution evidence. All 36 clean controls passed, and the complete strategy detected all 21 project--subject--fault clusters. This closes the controlled three-project count, although the NumPy and SciPy arms do not test autodiff and none of these wrappers is a field defect.

\subsection{Numerical gate coverage}
The registry contains four backend records and six required dimensions: value, gradient, calibration, module reuse, OOD, and long horizon. We additionally reran the MLSL backend through the P1 public factory API using the stable hybrid configuration. On the declared one-dimensional Dirichlet, negative-real spectral regime, the maximum relative value error against an 80-digit reference series was $1.32\times10^{-12}$; alpha and beta gradient errors against central differences were $2.22\times10^{-9}$ and $5.00\times10^{-10}$. Joint calibration recovered $(\alpha,\beta)=(0.78003,1.80007)$ from $(0.90,2.10)$ initialization. MLSL meets all six gates on this regime and retains the stated operating restrictions. All four records are \emph{conformant with declared scope limits}; their machine-readable status is \texttt{CONFORMANT}, and the restrictions remain part of each decision record.

\subsection{Task-level transfer and OOD evidence}
We additionally evaluated the structured primitive on two provenance-aware public datasets. For the GeoTES pilot-scale thermocouple histories (Zenodo DOI \href{https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.18979098}{10.5281/zenodo.18979098}), the first measured heating/cooling cycle was used for identification and the second cycle for transfer. Across three seeds, the DFSC-plus-residual model obtained a cycle-2 relative error of $0.3619\pm0.0085$, compared with $0.3632\pm0.0071$ for a pure MLP, using 750 rather than 1251 parameters. For the heated-steam temperature profiles (Zenodo DOI \href{https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.15064388}{10.5281/zenodo.15064388}), held-out-condition RMSE was $43.33\pm0.56$ K for direct DFSC, $16.03\pm0.14$ K for the hybrid, and $13.25\pm1.45$ K for the pure MLP. The latter result is retained as a negative task-level finding: a reliable primitive does not imply unconditional predictive superiority. Within the \texttt{Application} profile, these datasets test whether task evidence and operating restrictions are carried into the same record as component-level conformance. The negative result is operationally useful because it prevents a conformant numerical component from being reported as a superior predictive model without matched task evidence.

To separate observation density from extrapolation-horizon effects, we also repeated the GeoTES experiment with
uniform subsampling over the full first cycle while retaining the second cycle as a fixed holdout. At 40\% coverage,
the hybrid reached $0.3607\pm0.0082$ versus $0.3657\pm0.0084$ for the pure MLP, again with 750 versus 1251
parameters. The hybrid did not improve the MLP at 20\%, 60\%, or 100\% coverage. Its benefit is confined to the moderate-data setting observed here; Figure~\ref{fig:real-data-evidence} reports both public-data comparisons.

\paragraph{Module-level OOD evidence.}
In the common neural task, the Logistic primitive improves OOD RMSE relative to a pure MLP at both target times and both noise levels: for example, at $t=1.5$ and noise $0.003$, the primitive gives $0.2340\pm0.0323$ versus $0.2713\pm0.0096$ for the MLP. The matrix primitive does not improve this task: at the same setting it gives $0.1998\pm0.0272$ versus $0.1638\pm0.0097$. Figure~\ref{fig:common-ood} shows the full matched comparison. Training with a primitive is also slower in this implementation. The matched outcomes show that composition benefit depends on the task and the identifiable latent structure.

The negative matrix result does not originate from failure of the matrix exponential evaluator. In this task, the structured model must infer a sample-dependent $2\times2$ generator $A$ from sparse trajectory snapshots and then predict through $\exp(tA)y_0$. The MLP predicts the target state directly. The structured route adds a latent-parameter identification problem to state prediction. For a diagonalizable generator $A=V\Lambda V^{-1}$,
\begin{equation}
 \|\exp(tA)\|\leq \kappa(V)\exp\!\left(t\max_i\operatorname{Re}\lambda_i\right),
 \label{eq:matrix-sensitivity}
\end{equation}
so weakly damped modes and an ill-conditioned eigenvector basis can amplify errors in an inferred generator; nonnormal transient growth can make eigenvalues alone insufficient. We tested this explanation over 12 seed--time--noise configurations and correlated sample-wise OOD error with inferred-generator diagnostics. The mean Pearson correlation between log eigenvector condition number and log state error was $-0.044$, and the mean Spearman correlation was $-0.050$. Eigenvector conditioning alone does not explain the failure. Nonnormality and latent-parameter error had modest positive mean correlations of $0.305$ and $0.290$, respectively.

We also tested a prospective local-observability screen. For the three observed states, we formed the Jacobian of $[exp(t_1A)y_0;exp(t_2A)y_0;exp(t_3A)y_0]$ with respect to the four entries of $A$. Neither its smallest singular value nor its condition number supplied the expected monotone warning: the mean Pearson and Spearman correlations between log smallest singular value and log state error were $0.46$ and $0.50$, while the mean Pearson correlation for the log Jacobian condition number was $-0.19$. A single scalar threshold is therefore unsupported by these data. The audit records local observability, perturbation sensitivity, nonnormality, and parameter-fit diagnostics together; an adverse combination triggers targeted perturbation and matched-baseline tests rather than an automatic rejection. Passing value and gradient gates establishes evaluator reliability on the declared domain, but it does not resolve a poorly identified latent inverse problem.

\section{Discussion}
The standards mapping gives the proposal a defined role between numerical analysis and software test processes. ISO/IEC 25010 and 25023 motivate the quality characteristics and measurable fields; ISO/IEC/IEEE 29119-2 motivates the frozen test process and completion decision; IEEE 1012 motivates traceability from requirements to independent evidence. Together, these mappings provide traceable provenance for the component-level specification and its executable tests.

The mutation results show that the current rules detected all ten fault classes represented in the experiment, including an unfrozen scope and insufficient profile coverage. The paired strategy comparison adds a more specific conclusion: the envelope plus value/gradient strategy leaves batch, OOD, execution-policy, and frozen-scope defects invisible, whereas each added evidence layer closes declared fault classes without increasing false rejection on the clean controls. This is a comparative result for the frozen catalogue, not a claim that the complete record subsumes all testing methods. The Wilson intervals describe repeated tests of this implementation and mutation model; they do not estimate field-defect prevalence or prove completeness. Further mutation operators should cover malformed units, adversarial provenance, partial device fallback, stochastic nondeterminism, and cross-version semantic drift. Independent implementations are also needed to test whether the schema is sufficiently precise without relying on this reference code.

The external-subject ordering and complete PyTorch \#80770 and SciPy \#8906/\#15620 pairs improve realism without closing external validity. The matched subset reaches three independently maintained projects, but each project contributes only one interface to that subset and the NumPy/SciPy arms omit autodiff. Repeated seeds and representations remain within-family trials, not independent projects or defects. Three real families now cover a wrong derivative, an indexing exception, and silent dtype-dependent output, removing the earlier two-family limitation. They still span only two projects, however, so a JSS-level field-effectiveness claim requires broader historical replication.

The trajectory experiment connects the component tests to repeated simulation.
Value and gradient audits characterize one call; repeated application additionally
depends on the time horizon and step-size error budget. Figure~\ref{fig:simulation-benchmark}
quantifies this dependence for the controlled propagators studied here. Nonlinear,
stiff, and complex-geometry systems require corresponding trajectory tests before
the same qualification state can be assigned.
The experiments cover controlled low-dimensional components, one structured two-dimensional periodic PDE, two public-data transfer tests, and PyTorch execution. They show that the protocol can be applied to different numerical structures. They do not cover variable- or distributed-order operators, nonlinear or stiff PDEs, complex geometries, or unstructured meshes. Extending the protocol to those cases requires four additions: mesh and geometry metadata in the operating domain; an independent control based on manufactured solutions, mesh refinement, or a distinct discretization; convergence records for nonlinear solves and preconditioners; and horizon-dependent value, gradient, and conservation gates. These additions define new qualification studies rather than extending the present \texttt{CONFORMANT} decisions by assumption. JAX and Julia also require separate device and autodiff validation. The $2\times2$ matrix experiment exposes an interaction between weak identifiability and nonnormality, but does not quantify its growth in larger systems.

For higher-dimensional simulation, the audit budget must scale with the discretization. Let $n$ be the state dimension, $B$ the batch size, $K$ the number of value/reference cases, and $q$ the number of directional-gradient probes. If one primitive and reference evaluation cost $C_P(n)$ and $C_R(n)$, respectively, the leading work is approximately $K[C_P(n)+C_R(n)]+q[C_{P,\mathrm{AD}}(n)+2C_R(n)]$.

Directional probes avoid materializing a full parameter Jacobian. Live-state storage is at least $O(Bn)$, while reverse-mode graph storage depends on the primitive. The executable specification records chunk size, sparse structure, checkpointing, and peak device memory.

The independent reference may dominate the cost. A sparse finite-element primitive can use Krylov actions, while its control may require mesh refinement, a higher-order element, or a distinct discretization. Nonlinear and stiff problems add converged nonlinear solves, Jacobian or preconditioner construction, and trajectory-level references. Mesh-free primitives add dependence on the number of collocation points and residual derivative order.

When no closed form exists, manufactured solutions, conservation balances, and refinement studies can serve as controls. A reference built from the same discretization must be marked as non-independent. The periodic heat experiment applies this design to 65,536 spatial states and records grid size, value error, gradient error, synchronized latency, and peak memory. Navier--Stokes, stiff multiphysics, nonperiodic boundaries, and unstructured finite-element meshes remain separate qualification studies.

\subsection{Transfer to other component classes and standardization path}
The transferable part of the proposal is the record boundary, not a universal set of numerical gates. Database kernels, simulation modules, and AI inference components could reuse schema identity, version migration, provenance, requested-versus-observed execution fields, canonical serialization, and qualification outcomes. Each class would still need its own evidence plugin, independent control, tolerances, mutation operators, and operating-domain metadata. The experiments in this paper qualify the four studied numerical backends only; they do not establish conformance criteria for those other component classes.

Progress from a research specification to a consensus standard would require normative field definitions, public conformance examples, at least one independent implementation, and round-robin tests across organizations. A standards process would also need governance for controlled vocabularies, version deprecation, security and provenance requirements, and procedures for resolving incompatible interpretations. The released schema and artifacts provide executable inputs for such evaluation and interoperability studies.

\subsection{Component selection in SciML workflows}
A structured primitive is a reasonable choice when its parameters are identifiable from the available observations, an independent reference exercises the declared domain, and its value and gradient gates remain stable over the intended horizon. Task-level benefit must still be compared with an unstructured control.

Sparse or noisy observations can make the latent inverse problem underdetermined. Nonnormal or stiff dynamics can amplify transient errors and reverse-mode Jacobian products. A same-path reference provides weaker evidence than an independently constructed one. In these cases the qualification record remains \texttt{CONFORMANT} only when every mandatory gate passes on the restricted domain; otherwise it is \texttt{NONCONFORMANT}. Perturbation and identifiability diagnostics, together with a residual hybrid or direct learned baseline, explain the decision. A failed gate is a qualification result, not a software exception.

\section{Conclusion}
We proposed and implemented an executable testing and qualification method for differentiable numerical components. On a paired ten-class catalogue, fault-class coverage increased from 3/10 for envelope plus value/gradient checks to 5/10 for numerical properties, 8/10 for execution-aware checks, and 10/10 for the complete record. The same ordering reached 25.00\%, 73.75\%, 99.17\%, and 100\% detection on 240 external PyTorch behavior-fault trials. A matched NumPy/SciPy/PyTorch subset reached 28.57\%, 57.14\%, 85.71\%, and 100\% on 252 trials, closing the controlled three-project gate. Representation equivalence derived from SciPy \#8906 detected 32/32 variants missed by a weak correct example. Unchanged runners distinguish official buggy and current fixed behavior for PyTorch \#80770 and SciPy \#8906/\#15620, completing three historical pairs across two projects. The method therefore connects incremental evidence to external interfaces and real release-level defects while remaining a falsifiable component-level argument rather than a universal reliability guarantee.
